# AML/CFT Supervisory Expectations for Digital Payment Token Service Providers

**Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS)** **July 2026**

---

## Introduction

This information paper sets out MAS' observations and supervisory expectations to guide digital payment token service providers (DPTSPs) in implementing their anti-money laundering, countering the financing of terrorism, and countering proliferation financing (AML/CFT/CPF) controls in key areas.

DPTSPs in Singapore are generally aware of their AML/CFT/CPF obligations and have established AML/CFT/CPF frameworks and controls. Nonetheless, there remains room for improvement in the implementation and execution of certain key AML/CFT/CPF controls, as elaborated in this paper. DPTSPs should benchmark themselves against the practices and supervisory expectations set out in this paper, in a risk-based and proportionate manner.

DPTSPs should conduct a gap analysis, taking into account the risk profile of the entity at the enterprise level (e.g. business operations/activities, products and customers, etc.). Where DPTSPs identify gaps in their AML/CFT/CPF frameworks and controls, specific remediation/enhancement measures should be implemented on a timely basis. The Board and senior management should exercise close oversight of the gap assessment and root cause analysis, and ensure effective implementation of follow-up actions.

This information paper supplements existing AML/CFT/CPF requirements and should be read in conjunction with MAS Notice PSN02 and accompanying Guidelines.

This information paper sets out MAS' supervisory expectations in the areas of:
1. **Assessment of Risks Arising from New Products** (including DPTs¹)
2. **Enhanced Customer Due Diligence Measures on Higher Risk Customers**
3. **Value Transfer Requirements**
4. **Ongoing Monitoring**
5. **Screening**
6. **Due Diligence on Partners and Outsourced Providers**
7. **Training and Staff Expertise**

*¹ DPTs refer to Digital Payment Tokens, as defined in section 2 of the Payment Services Act 2019.*

## 1. Assessment of Risks Arising from New Products

### Supervisory Expectations

DPTSPs should have a formalised approach to identify and assess money laundering²/terrorism financing (ML/TF) risks before offering new products (including the offering and listing of new DPTs on their platforms), in accordance with paragraph 5 of MAS Notice PSN02. Such an assessment should be comprehensive and consider both quantitative and qualitative factors, including the risk indicators listed in paragraph 5-3 of the Guidelines to Notice PSN02.

To further supplement those risk indicators, the following should be considered prior to offering and/or listing of new DPTs:

* **(a) ML/TF risks directly associated with the DPT**, including the DPT's association with higher risk persons, entities, or jurisdictions; and whether the DPTSP's compliance tools can effectively trace and monitor transactions involving the DPT;
* **(b) Track record of the DPT**, including the reputation, credibility, and any regulatory record of the DPT, its creators, and its governance mechanism. Such an assessment should also include a consideration of adverse news coverage, an evaluation of the issuer, and the ownership and holding concentration of the DPT;
* **(c) Market and economic indicators** to identify risks of manipulation and fraud, including assessing the DPT's (i) trading supply, volume, and liquidity; (ii) market support and trading patterns; and (iii) built-in inflationary or deflationary mechanisms; and
* **(d) Technological and cybersecurity risks**, particularly vulnerabilities arising from the DPT's smart contract and underlying blockchain.

While not all the above contribute directly to ML/TF risks, each represents a potential vulnerability that illicit actors could exploit to facilitate ML/TF activities. DPTSPs must therefore evaluate these interconnected risks holistically to prevent their platforms from being misused for illicit purposes.

*² Includes proliferation financing.*

### Key Observations

While DPTSPs generally have policies and procedures (P&Ps) in place to assess risks arising from new products, there were areas that can be improved. These include providing more granular and specific guidance on the risk indicators to be considered, ensuring that relevant controls are documented in the P&Ps and risk assessments, and conducting ongoing monitoring of DPTs (e.g. via screening and monitoring of trading volume and adverse news etc.) to ensure that appropriate risk mitigating actions are taken where required. Most DPTSPs have also adopted a cautious approach to the offering and listing of DPTs and have focused mainly on a limited set of DPTs with lower risks.

#### I. Granularity and Clarity of P&Ps and Risk Assessments
* While DPTSPs generally have a set of broad ML/TF risk indicators to guide their assessment as to whether new DPTs should be offered to customers or listed on their platform, some did not provide adequately specific guidance to staff on how the said risk indicators should be assessed, including the information sources that should be considered.
* Some P&Ps also lacked clarity on the key parties (e.g. business, compliance, technology risk functions, etc.) that should be involved in the assessment and review process. DPTSPs should ensure that their P&Ps provide sufficient clarity so that staff are aware of their roles and responsibilities, the risks that they should look out for, and mitigating measures to be considered.
* Risk assessments should also be well-documented. Notwithstanding that most of a DPTSP's listed DPTs could be well-known, DPTSPs should clearly document any deviations from their P&Ps, including cases where the DPTs do not meet their risk appetite, along with the relevant justifications.
* DPTSPs should also have a governance process to ensure that the appropriate compliance/control functions sign off on the risk assessments, and that approvals from senior management are obtained and properly documented.

#### II. Formalised Process for Ongoing Monitoring
* Some DPTSPs did not document measures for ongoing monitoring for offered/listed DPTs in their P&Ps, including adverse news monitoring.
* DPTSPs should formalise their process for ongoing monitoring of offered DPTs and ensure that adverse news is monitored, the potential impact of such news is properly assessed, and that these assessments are documented. This is important to ensure that DPTSPs continue to offer only DPTs that are within their ML/TF risk appetite.

### Case Studies

#### Case Study A
DPTSP A's P&Ps required it to assess various ML/TF risk indicators prior to listing a DPT. However, the P&Ps were not sufficiently comprehensive as the reasons supporting the relevant thresholds for the various DPT assessment criteria (such as minimum market capitalisation and average daily traded volume) were not documented. The reasons for the thresholds and criteria should have been documented to facilitate periodic reviews and to provide clarity to staff in relation to the risks that should be mitigated.

Some of the controls implemented by the DPTSP, such as daily news checks on its listed DPTs, were also not documented in the P&Ps. Proper documentation of key controls in the P&Ps is important to ensure that they are formalised and implemented on an ongoing basis.

#### Case Study B
DPTSP B did not have a formalised framework to identify and assess ML/TF risks before offering/listing new DPTs on its platform. Its DPT risk assessments were based on a list of criteria that included ML/TF risk indicators, but did not include sufficient guidance on how the indicators were considered in totality.

While DPTSP B represented that all its DPT risk assessments and decisions on whether to offer/list certain DPTs were undertaken by its senior management and approved at the Board, these risk assessments and senior management/board approvals were not documented. Hence, there was no evidence that DPTSP B had indeed assessed the ML/TF risks of the DPTs before they were offered/listed.

Although DPTSP B represented that it would, on an ongoing basis, monitor for any adverse news relating to the DPTs that it had offered/listed, these measures were not documented in its P&Ps. There was also no evidence that DPTSP B had indeed performed the ongoing monitoring of adverse news as represented.

## 2. Enhanced Customer Due Diligence Measures

### Supervisory Expectations

As part of enhanced customer due diligence (ECDD) measures for politically-exposed persons (PEPs) and higher risk customers, DPTSPs are required to:

* **(a) Obtain approval from senior management** to establish or continue business relations.
* **(b) Establish customers' and their beneficial owners' source of wealth (SOW) and source of funds (SOF):**
  * In establishing SOF where incoming funds are DPTs, insights from blockchain analytics and/or other surveillance tools should be used to augment/complement other sources of off-chain information to form a holistic view of customer risks.
  * Where a customer's SOW is derived from virtual asset activities, DPTSPs should seek to understand the customer's virtual asset exposure including the type and nature of virtual asset transactions and holdings. This should include obtaining relevant supporting documents to corroborate the customer's virtual asset transactions and holdings.
* **(c) Conduct enhanced monitoring** of the business relations with the customer e.g. subjecting the customer to a higher frequency of periodic review and/or enhanced transaction monitoring rules.

### Key Observations

#### I. Gaps in Customer Risk Assessment Frameworks
* Some DPTSPs did not adequately consider both on-chain and off-chain risk information in their customer risk assessment frameworks.
* There were also instances where the DPTSP's P&Ps lacked clarity on its approach and the risk indicators that should be considered/assessed to detect PEPs and higher risk customers.

#### II. Inadequate Conduct of ECDD measures for Higher Risk Customers
* Some DPTSPs mainly relied on customers' representations and did not obtain relevant independent supporting documents to establish and assess the plausibility of customers' SOW and SOF.
* In some cases, DPTSPs failed to seek senior management approval to establish business relations with higher risk customers.

### Case Studies

#### Case Study C
DPTSP C represented that it used regular on-chain screening of customer transactions to determine its customers' exposure to higher risk activities, and monitor for any triggers of other higher risk indicators such as adverse news, unusual transactions and attempted third-party value transfers.

However, its P&Ps did not comprehensively include the list of ML/TF risk indicators that it monitored, nor any guidance on how the higher risk indicators would be assessed and addressed. The DPTSP's P&Ps also did not adequately state its approach to assess the jurisdiction risks of its customers, and there was no information on how higher risk jurisdictions are determined.

As a result, the DPTSP's staff had only considered the current nationality of the customer but did not assess the fact that the customer originally held the nationality of a higher-risk jurisdiction, and/or had derived his source of wealth/funds from that said higher-risk jurisdiction.

#### Case Study D
DPTSP D did not demonstrate a good understanding of SOW and SOF concepts. The DPTSP only focused on establishing the existence of the customers' wealth and funds (e.g. using bank statements), without gaining a deeper understanding of the activities that generated the material sources of wealth and funds injected into the customers' accounts, for corroboration purposes.

DPTSP D also did not obtain from its higher risk customers a base set of SOW information, such as the estimated size of the customers' overall wealth, at onboarding. DPTSP D was therefore unable to adequately establish the SOW of its customers and assess whether the customers' transactions were in line with its understanding of the customers.

#### Case Study E
DPTSP E's P&Ps did not require staff to establish the SOF and SOW of the beneficial owners of higher risk customers. They also allowed the SOF assessment of its higher risk customers to be based solely on the customers' own declarations. These practices are not in line with the requirements set out in MAS Notice PSN02.

As a result, DPTSP E onboarded higher risk customers without establishing the SOW and SOF of their beneficial owners. DPTSP E also onboarded a higher risk customer without adequately establishing the SOF of the customer, even when the customer declared an expected account activity that reflected amounts significantly higher than the customer's known annual income. In both cases, senior management had approved the establishment of business relations with these customers despite the obvious gaps in the ECDD measures performed.

## 3. Value Transfer Requirements

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF)'s Travel Rule requires virtual asset service providers (VASPs) and financial institutions to obtain, hold, and transmit specific originator and beneficiary information immediately and securely when transferring virtual assets (VAs).

Globally, jurisdictions have made progress in implementing the Travel Rule. From the 2025 FATF Targeted Update on Virtual Assets, 73% of respondents (85 of 117 jurisdictions, excluding those that prohibit or plan to explicitly prohibit VASPs) have passed legislation implementing the Travel Rule. Despite this, there are still considerable gaps in Travel Rule implementation globally. Some jurisdictions have indicated that global implementation has not been fully achieved, and VAs and VASPs remain vulnerable to misuse.

### Supervisory Expectations

MAS Notice PSN02 implements the Travel Rule, requiring that when DPT value transfers are made between DPTSPs on behalf of their customers, the originating DPTSP must transmit originator and beneficiary information to the beneficiary DPTSP, concurrently or simultaneously with the value transfer, in a secure manner. This is to (a) ensure necessary identification information to facilitate screening; and (b) provide an audit / traceability trail for investigators when needed.

Enhanced risk mitigation measures should be taken when value transfers are made to, and/or received from, unhosted wallets or wallets hosted on unregulated VASPs. Risk mitigation measures that DPTSPs have taken include:
* Permitting and verifying that only first-party incoming/outgoing transfers are processed; and
* Requiring customers to demonstrate ownership and control of wallets, which may entail conducting a Satoshi test, signing a message with the wallet address, or sighting the signing-in of the wallet via video conference.

This is to ensure that (a) the higher ML/TF risks associated with such transfers are duly mitigated; and (b) necessary counterparty information is obtained to facilitate screening.

#### Guiding Factors for Evaluation of Travel Rule Solution
When selecting a suitable Travel Rule solution, DPTSPs should adopt a comprehensive evaluation framework, which minimally considers the following factors³:
* **(a) Coverage:** The DPT types and network of VASPs that are covered by the solution;
* **(b) Interoperability:** The solution’s interoperability with other Travel Rule compliance tools, and any limitations of the solution in sending/receiving data to/from the counterparty VASP;
* **(c) VASP due diligence measures:** The rigour of due diligence measures that the solution applies to VASPs that are allowed to be onboarded as members to its network;
* **(d) Counterparty VASP identification and due diligence measures:** The robustness of entity verification and contact information validation for network member VASPs and counterparty VASPs that are not within its network but allowed to interface with its network members;
* **(e) Immediate transmission of data:** The solution’s technical ability to facilitate the transmission of value transfer information simultaneously or concurrently with the value transfer; and
* **(f) Data security and protection:** The comprehensiveness of security safeguards implemented by the solution to protect value transfer information from unauthorised disclosure.

DPTSPs should be mindful of the limitations of their chosen Travel Rule solution(s) and take steps to mitigate the attendant risks.

*³ DPTSPs can also refer to the 2024 Targeted Update on Implementation of the FATF Standards on VAs and VASPs which provides several guiding questions for VASPs to consider when evaluating Travel Rule Solutions.*

### Key Observations

#### Inadequate Screening Controls for Value Transfers
* Some DPTSPs did not implement adequate controls to ensure that originators and beneficiaries of value transfers were screened before effecting the transfers, potentially exposing those DPTSPs to ML/TF risk.
* In Singapore, most DPTSPs adopt Travel Rule solutions to comply with the value transfer requirements, with ~25% of DPTSPs using more than 1 Travel Rule solution.
* As part of their evaluation of Travel Rule solutions, most DPTSPs considered the solution’s VA and counterparty coverage, interoperability, and reputation. In addition to these factors, DPTSPs should also consider the factors listed under the preceding section: “Guiding Factors for Evaluation of Travel Rule Solution”.
* Most DPTSPs have also implemented enhanced risk mitigation measures for transfers to and/or from unhosted wallets or wallets hosted on unregulated VASPs.

### Case Studies

#### Case Study F
DPTSP F allowed customers to make value transfers to a recipient’s (either first-party or third-party) unhosted wallets or wallets hosted by another DPTSP. DPTSP F implemented a Travel Rule solution to comply with the value transfer requirements. However, the solution only covered a very small proportion of DPTSP F’s value transfers. For the majority of value transfers, DPTSP F did not provide the required originator and beneficiary information to the beneficiary DPTSPs because the solution either did not support the transferred DPT or the counterparty DPTSP was not in the solution’s network.

For value transfers to DPTSPs not within the solution’s network, DPTSP F only took risk mitigation measures if the customer’s transactions were associated with high-risk entities as detected by their blockchain analytics tools. This was not in line with MAS’ expectations⁴ which require effective risk mitigation measures to be applied for all value transfers which are not compliant with the value transfer requirements.

Similarly, for value transfers to unhosted wallets, DPTSP F did not require enhanced risk mitigation measures to be performed for such transfers even though they posed inherently higher ML/TF risks. Instead, DPTSP F only performed them if the customer’s transactions were associated with high-risk entities as detected by their blockchain analytics tools. This was not in line with MAS’ Notice PSN02 requirements for enhanced risk mitigation measures to be performed on all value transfers to and from unhosted wallets.

*⁴ As set out in MAS guidance issued in March 2021 on Strengthening AML/CFT Controls of Digital Payment Token Service Providers.*

### Counterparty Due Diligence

* DPTSPs should seek to understand the risks of dealing with a counterparty VASP. In this regard, DPTSPs should conduct due diligence on their counterparty VASPs to ensure that they are not dealing with illicit actors or sanctioned actors.
* This includes understanding whether the counterparty VASP:
  * **(a)** is licensed/registered and supervised for AML/CFT in a jurisdiction that has implemented the FATF Standards for VAs/VASPs; and
  * **(b)** has been the subject of any regulatory or enforcement action.
* DPTSPs are expected to conduct an independent risk assessment of the counterparty VASP. The assessment should be performed in a risk-based manner and could involve reviewing the counterparty’s AML/CFT controls framework to ensure that the counterparty VASP is able to comply with the Travel Rule and will securely receive, retain, and safeguard Travel Rule information.
* Some Travel Rule solution providers conduct due diligence on the counterparty VASPs that are allowed to use the solution to interface with their network members and make such information available. However, the level and robustness of the due diligence performed on these counterparty VASPs and the relevant information available on the VASPs vary across solution providers. Consequently, while such information can inform a DPTSP’s risk assessment of the counterparty VASP, it should not be the sole information that the DPTSP relies on.
* DPTSPs should assess if they are able to manage the risks of dealing with the counterparty VASPs. For counterparty VASPs that present higher ML/TF risks, DPTSPs should take reasonable risk mitigation measures.
* This includes:
  * **(a)** performing more frequent due diligence reviews;
  * **(b)** conducting enhanced monitoring of value transfers with the counterparty VASP; and
  * **(c)** imposing transaction limits.
* DPTSPs do not need to undertake counterparty VASP due diligence for every value transfer with the same counterparty VASP. However, DPTSPs should ensure effective ongoing monitoring of their counterparty VASPs and refresh their counterparty due diligence information periodically or when risks emerge from the relationship, to determine if they remain comfortable submitting Travel Rule information to the counterparty VASP.
* If the DPTSP cannot mitigate and manage the ML/TF risks posed by transacting with the counterparty VASP, it should refrain from executing or facilitating the value transfer.
* DPTSPs should take steps to ensure that Travel Rule information is not sent to the wrong counterparty/beneficiary VASP, illicit actors, or sanctioned parties. In particular:
  * **(a)** DPTSPs should not solely rely on the Travel Rule solution for the identification and verification of the counterparty/beneficiary VASP. DPTSPs should independently confirm the owner of the beneficiary wallet address, such as through customer declaration or confirmation with the beneficiary VASP;
  * **(b)** DPTSPs should not send Travel Rule information to unverified email addresses of counterparty/beneficiary VASPs; and
  * **(c)** DPTSPs should take appropriate security arrangements to prevent any unauthorised access to Travel Rule information.

## 4. Ongoing Monitoring

### Supervisory Expectations

DPTSPs are required to monitor business relations with customers on an ongoing basis, and ensure that transactions in both DPT and fiat are taken into consideration when assessing whether customers’ behaviour is consistent with the DPTSPs’ knowledge of the customers, their business and risk profiles, and their SOW/SOF.

DPTSPs are also required to pay particular attention to all complex, unusually large or unusual patterns of DPT and fiat transactions undertaken, that have no apparent or visible economic or lawful purpose. This includes:
* **(a)** establishing parameters and scenarios to identify suspicious transaction patterns and the use of anonymity-related services;
* **(b)** enhanced monitoring for higher risk situations; and
* **(c)** utilising data and blockchain analytics tools to enhance the tracing and detection of suspicious transactions.

While the use of blockchain analytics tools is useful in the screening of wallet addresses and tracing of DPTs on the blockchain, DPTSPs should not solely rely on it for transaction monitoring or identifying risk exposure posed by wallet addresses.

As part of ongoing monitoring, DPTSPs should also monitor DPT transactional patterns to detect behavioural risk posed by customers’ DPT transactions. This should be implemented alongside fiat transaction monitoring.

DPTSPs should have robust systems and processes to develop a holistic view of their customers’ transactions and identify unusual patterns of DPT and fiat transactions (such as deposit and withdrawal transactions that are inconsistent with the customers’ profiles) for further review. This includes:
* **(a)** Setting appropriate parameters and thresholds to flag unusual DPT and fiat transactions that warrant further inquiry, taking into account their specific risks and context. Where DPTSPs use external or intragroup transaction monitoring systems or tools, DPTSPs should not merely adopt default or group thresholds, rules, parameters and scenarios without reviewing and ensuring appropriateness to their business model, customers’ profile, transaction patterns and risk appetite;
* **(b)** Periodically reviewing the appropriateness of parameters and thresholds to ensure they remain current and effective;
* **(c)** Ensuring clear procedures and guidance for staff to assess, escalate or dismiss alerts generated by their systems; and
* **(d)** Conducting quality assurance to assess the quality of their alert handling process to detect and rectify any weaknesses observed.

### Key Observations

#### I. Lapses in Ongoing Monitoring of Customers’ Transactions
* MAS noted several lapses in DPTSPs’ ongoing monitoring of customers’ transactions. For instance, in some cases, DPTSPs did not conduct adequate follow-up with customers on unusually large transactions to ensure that they were consistent with their knowledge of these customers.
* While most DPTSPs utilised blockchain analytics tools to detect potentially higher risk DPT transactions, some DPTSPs did not perform a sufficiently thorough assessment of the ML/TF and sanction risks posed before clearing those alerts.
* DPTSPs have mainly employed blockchain analytics tools to detect potentially suspicious/higher risk DPT transactions. They were able to use such tools to identify and analyse direct or indirect exposure to entities associated with higher risks, including darknet markets, fraud shops, high risk exchanges, high risk jurisdictions, mixers/tumblers and scams, so that timely risk mitigation measures can be taken.
* Even with the use of blockchain analytics tools, DPTSPs are reminded to customise the risk parameters/rules within the tool, as appropriate, to be aligned with their ML/TF risk appetite and compliance policies.

### Case Studies

#### Case Study G
DPTSP G did not implement appropriate parameters or thresholds to identify unusual patterns of DPT and fiat transactions, such as frequent inflows of DPTs followed by fast outflows of fiat transactions within a short period of time. Consequently, DPTSP G failed to inquire into such transaction patterns, which were inconsistent with its customer’s declared account purpose and anticipated transaction volume, and did not take appropriate risk mitigation measures where required.

#### Case Study H
DPTSP H’s compliance function was required to identify any deposit and withdrawal transactions that were inconsistent with customers’ profiles for further review. There were some instances where the amount of DPTs deposited by customers were significantly higher than their known annual income levels. However, DPTSP H did not obtain sufficient information to ascertain whether these customers’ transactions were consistent with its knowledge of their annual income levels.

There were also some instances where DPTSP H’s customers’ whitelisted unhosted wallets had indirect exposures to sanctioned entities, as identified by its blockchain analytics tool. However, DPTSP H’s assessment was that the ML/TF risks were acceptable merely based on the fact that the amounts received by DPTSP H were small.

DPTSP H should have conducted a more thorough assessment of the ML/TF risks posed by the transactions, but it did not do so as it did not have additional on-chain information, such as the time stamps of these on-chain transactions and the counterparties of intermediary wallets between the sanctioned wallet addresses and the screened wallet address. Such additional information would have helped DPTSP H to better assess the potential ML/TF risks, including whether high-velocity transactions or layering in relation to financial sanctions and illicit funds had taken place.

## 5. Screening

### Supervisory Expectations

Screening is a fundamental control within AML/CFT/CPF frameworks and serves as a preventative control. DPTSPs are required to screen customers and their relevant parties⁵ against relevant ML/TF information sources, as well as lists and information provided by MAS or other relevant authorities in Singapore. This enables DPTSPs to identify any potential risk nexus to sanctions, PEPs, and other adverse information, thereby allowing them to better assess and effectively mitigate the attendant ML/TF risks.

DPTSPs should review the underlying data sources of their screening systems and/or tools to ensure that all relevant ML/TF and targeted financial sanctions information sources are included and updated in the screening databases in a timely manner. For further guidance on supervisory expectations relating to name screening, including the adequacy of information sources, DPTSPs may refer to the information paper published by the MAS in April 2022 on Strengthening AML/CFT Name Screening Practices.

*⁵ Relevant parties would include any natural persons appointed to act on behalf of the customer, connected parties of the customer, beneficial owners of the customer, and value transfer originators and beneficiaries.*

### Key Observations

* DPTSPs were generally familiar with their sanctions obligations and had adopted screening tools to screen customers and their relevant parties against appropriate sanctions lists at onboarding and on an ongoing basis. Blockchain analytics tools were also used to detect exposure to wallet addresses with nexus to sanctions risks.

#### Deficiencies in ML/TF Screening of Customers and Poor Oversight and Execution of Name Screening
* Some DPTSPs were unfamiliar with their screening vendors’ databases and did not assess if the information sources used in the databases were adequate.
* In addition, some DPTSPs had gaps in their screening processes and failed to screen relevant parties on a timely basis. These weaknesses increase the risks of DPTSPs dealing with illicit or sanctioned parties.

### Case Studies

#### Case Study I
DPTSP I did not perform name screening on all value transfer originators and value transfer beneficiaries as required under MAS Notice PSN02. DPTSP I explained that it met the requirements under MAS Notice PSN02 as it only allowed first-party transfers and had performed name screening of the customers at onboarding and annually thereafter. Moreover, its customers’ whitelisted wallet addresses, and all DPT transactions, would be screened by its blockchain analytics tool to avoid dealing with sanctioned wallet addresses.

Although the measures taken by DPTSP I could reduce the risks of dealing with sanctioned persons, a material gap remained. To illustrate, a customer who passes name screening checks at onboarding will only be re-screened a year later. If this customer becomes sanctioned six months after onboarding, DPTSP I may not be aware of this until another six months later, by which time it may have inadvertently processed value transfers for the sanctioned customer.

#### Case Study J
DPTSP J did not engage its screening vendor to better understand the vendor’s underlying information databases (including coverage of adverse news) and assess their adequacy and robustness in meeting DPTSP J’s screening needs.

Gaps in DPTSP J’s screening vendor’s databases resulted in adverse news in relation to a customer being charged with corruption not being detected. DPTSP J has since terminated business relations with the customer given the adverse information.

## 6. Due Diligence on Partners and Outsourced AML/CFT Service Providers

### Supervisory Expectations

Many DPTSPs have varying commercial arrangements with other DPTSPs such as liquidity providers (Partners) and/or have outsourced certain AML/CFT functions either to intra-group entities or external service providers (Service Providers).

DPTSPs should conduct due diligence on their Partners and Service Providers before onboarding them, and periodically thereafter. DPTSPs are expected to assess, among others, the probity, ownership structure and business of their Partners and Service Providers, the robustness of their AML/CFT/CPF risk control frameworks, as well as alignment with the DPTSP’s own AML/CFT/CPF framework and regulatory requirements. Where relevant, DPTSPs should also consider the Partners and Service Providers’ audit findings when assessing their AML/CFT/CPF controls.

Where DPTSPs have outsourced their AML/CFT/CPF functions, the responsibility to comply with AML/CFT/CPF requirements remains with the DPTSPs. Consequently, DPTSPs should establish proper governance and maintain management oversight of their Service Providers.

DPTSPs are expected to establish control mechanisms to monitor and assess the performance of their Service Providers against clear and measurable key performance indicators. Regular quality assurance reviews and onsite visits on the Service Providers should be conducted to assess the ongoing effectiveness of the AML/CFT/CPF controls implemented, where relevant.

## 7. Training and Staff Expertise

### Supervisory Expectations

MAS expects DPTSPs to ensure that their staff and officers receive regular and appropriate training to effectively discharge their roles and responsibilities, including the DPTSPs’ AML/CFT/CPF obligations. The scope and frequency of training should be tailored to the specific risks faced by the DPTSPs and calibrated according to the job functions, responsibilities and experience of the staff and officers.

MAS observed that some staff such as technical blockchain experts demonstrated inadequate ML/TF risk awareness. In other instances, the DPTSPs’ AML/CFT/compliance staff did not have prior relevant sectoral experience and was not sufficiently familiar with specific risks in the DPT space.

It is important for DPTSPs to implement a comprehensive and tailored training programme to ensure that their staff are minimally equipped with a baseline understanding of blockchain technology, cryptocurrencies, and ML/TF risks posed by the DPTSPs’ products and services and associated regulatory developments.

DPTSPs are also expected to have a balanced representation of technical DPT, AML/CFT, and compliance experts within relevant committees to oversee decision-making for key frameworks and controls, such as the new product approval process.

## Conclusion

The DPT sector presents heightened ML/TF risks, given the pseudonymous and cross-border nature of DPT transactions. DPTSPs should therefore ensure that their AML/CFT/CPF risk detection capabilities and controls remain effective to detect, assess, and mitigate these risks.

Overall, MAS’ inspections of DPTSPs and ongoing engagements with various licence applicants and licensees indicate that there is room for DPTSPs to enhance their AML/CFT/CPF frameworks and implementation of related controls.

The Board and senior management play a critical role in supporting the effectiveness of these frameworks and controls. The Board and senior management should maintain close oversight of the ML/TF risks faced by DPTSPs and ensure that AML/CFT/CPF risk mitigation remains a key priority.

DPTSPs should assess the effectiveness of existing frameworks and controls against MAS’ observations and supervisory expectations set out in this information paper and take timely and risk-proportionate steps to address any identified gaps. DPTSPs should also ensure that relevant learnings are incorporated in their P&Ps and shared with staff to raise ML/TF risk awareness across the organisation.
